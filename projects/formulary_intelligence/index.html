Formulary Intelligence — coverage-aware pharmacotherapy recommendation

Clinical decision support · retrieval-augmented pipeline · schema-constrained reasoning

Healthcare Business Problem — An AI Agent Workflow called Formulary Intelligence that performs drug recommendations for patients' pharmacological actions, recommending only drugs covered by their insurance plan

Whenever a patient's clinical record includes a pharmacological action like "start an SGLT2 inhibitor to manage blood sugar," Formulary Intelligence generates the drug recommendation for it using LLM and RAG, limited to drugs covered by the patient's insurance plan. It does this by checking what the plan covers, identifying the drug class for that specific pharmacological action using GPT-4o, and searching that class within the plan's covered drug list using vector semantic search. It then uses an LLM to select the final list of relevant drugs that are both covered and in the same class, and the clinician prescribes from that list.

Worked example · Type 2 diabetes · “start metformin to manage blood sugar”

One action in the record, one prescription that clears.

Without a coverage check, the clinician prescribes blind. The drug may be non-preferred, may need prior authorisation, or may not be on the plan at all — and nobody finds out until the claim is rejected at the pharmacy counter, days later, with the patient standing there waiting for medication they were told to start.

Here is how it works for a patient with Type 2 diabetes whose clinical record includes the action “start metformin to manage blood sugar.”

  1. Check coverage. The system looks up the patient's insurance plan and finds their formulary, the official list of drugs that plan covers.
  2. Extract drug actions. It reads the clinical record and keeps only the medication-related actions. Here, that is “start metformin to manage blood sugar.” Referrals, monitoring plans and lifestyle counselling stay in the record, but never enter this pipeline.
  3. Identify drug class. An AI call understands that metformin belongs to the Biguanides drug class. This call sees no formulary data at all — it is asked only what clinical knowledge can answer, never what a particular plan happens to cover.
  4. Retrieve covered drugs. It searches the patient's plan for covered drugs in the Biguanides class and finds metformin immediate-release (500 mg, 850 mg and 1000 mg tablets) and metformin extended-release (500 mg, 750 mg and 1000 mg tablets). That is the whole list, because metformin is the only biguanide available for prescription today. Both are preferred, first-tier generics on this plan, with no prior authorisation and no step therapy required. The same retrieval works for any drug class. For SGLT2 inhibitors the list might show Jardiance, Farxiga, Invokana and Steglatro. For GLP-1 receptor agonists it might show Ozempic, Trulicity, Victoza and Mounjaro. For statins it might show atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin and pravastatin.
  5. Select relevant drugs. A second AI call picks the relevant ones from only that retrieved list. Both metformin forms come back as relevant. Because the model is handed that list and a schema that accepts entries from it, it has no way to name a drug this plan does not cover.
  6. Clinician prescribes. The doctor now sees the recommended drugs, metformin extended-release and metformin immediate-release, with their available strengths, and prescribes from this list, confident that the insurance will cover whichever one they choose.

In this example, the clinician writes the actual prescription for metformin extended-release 500 mg, one tablet once daily with the evening meal. Because it came from the recommended, coverage-aware list, the prescription goes through without a coverage rejection.

Prior authorisation and step therapy

Generic metformin IR and ER are almost always preferred, first-tier drugs on US formularies. They typically need no prior authorisation and no step therapy, because metformin is the standard first-line treatment for Type 2 diabetes and costs very little. Plans usually apply only simple quantity limits, such as a maximum number of tablets per month.

The branded extended-release versions — Fortamet and Glumetza — are the ones that can be non-preferred and require prior authorisation. That is exactly why coverage-aware recommendation matters: it steers the clinician toward the covered generic first.

Plan details, formulary contents and the patient in this example are synthetic. Dosing figures are standard prescribing references, included to show the shape of the data a clinician works from — not clinical guidance.
