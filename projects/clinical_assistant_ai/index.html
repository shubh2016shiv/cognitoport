Clinical Copilot Chat — grounded, streaming clinical question answering

Clinical decision support · retrieval-grounded generation · streaming response

Healthcare Business Problem — A clinician asks a question about the patient in front of them, and gets an answer grounded in that patient's record and in the clinical guidance that applies, streamed back while they read it

A clinician mid-encounter asks something like "her LDL is still 142 on atorvastatin 20 — what would you do next?" Answering it needs three things the question does not contain: what this patient's record actually says, what the relevant guideline actually recommends, and what was already said earlier in the conversation. This pipeline fetches all three — guidelines by hybrid search, the patient's record and the conversation history in parallel alongside it — stages them into one prompt, and streams the model's answer back over a channel that was already open before any of that work began. Sources are numbered before the model sees them, so the citations at the end record what the answer actually used.

Worked example · lipid management · “her LDL is still 142 on atorvastatin 20 — what would you do next?”

One question, and everything that has to happen before the first word.

The question contains almost nothing the system needs. It names a lab value and a drug, but not the patient, not their history, not what the guideline says about intensifying statin therapy, and not what the clinician asked two minutes ago. Answering it well means reconstructing all of that in under two seconds, while somebody watches.

Here is what happens between the clinician pressing enter and the first word appearing, for a synthetic patient with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

  1. Admit the question — about 0.03 seconds. The clinician's token is verified, the feature flags for this request are resolved, and identifiers are minted for the thread, the run and this turn. The question itself is written to the transcript before anything tries to answer it, so a failure thirty seconds from now still leaves a record of what was asked. Then the stream opens, and a status frame goes out immediately — everything after this point happens behind a connection that is already live.
  2. Two lanes start at once. Guideline retrieval and patient context assembly are fired together. Neither depends on the other, and neither depends on any reasoning, which is exactly why a planning step would be expensive here — it would make both of these conditional on its own output and forfeit the parallelism.
  3. Lane A: find the guidance — about 1.05 seconds. The question is embedded, then issued against the clinical guideline index as one hybrid query — keyword matching and vector similarity together, semantically reranked, capped at five passages. Back come excerpts on LDL targets for secondary prevention and on when to intensify therapy or add a non-statin agent. Each source document is stripped of any citation markers it already carried and assigned a number: source 1, source 2, source 3. The model will be told to cite by those numbers, and it will never see a source that was not numbered here.
  4. Lane B: load the patient and the conversation — about 1.22 seconds. The patient's clinical picture comes from a read-through cache in around twenty milliseconds: the LDL trend, the current atorvastatin 20 mg, prior intolerances, the ASCVD history, renal function. Alongside it, three pre-rendered care views. And alongside those, the slowest call in the entire request — the conversation history, at roughly 1.18 seconds, re-paired into strictly alternating turns with the reference blocks stripped out of previous answers. That one read sets the floor for this whole phase. Everything else in both lanes finishes inside its shadow.
  5. Stage one prompt — about a millisecond. The system prompt is fetched from the registry and the numbered guideline excerpts are substituted into it. The patient views are then staged as synthetic exchanges — the assistant appearing to have already stated the clinical picture — followed by the real conversation history, followed by the live question. Patient data enters as conversation, never as instruction.
  6. Buy capacity — about 0.27 seconds. The prompt is costed and a slot is acquired against the shared model quota, returning a metered client. This is a sequential network round trip sitting between finished context and the model call, on the critical path, and it is the second thing worth optimising: the token estimate does not actually depend on the context it is waiting behind.
  7. Stream the answer — first token at about 0.6 seconds, then roughly 1.3 seconds of generation. The first chunk arrives under a ten-second deadline and is inspected: it is content, not a tool call, so the answer has begun. As tokens flow past, citation markers are stripped inline and their identifiers recorded — including one that arrived split across three separate frames as a bracket, then a digit, then a closing bracket. The clinician starts reading at 1.9 seconds and keeps reading while the rest is still being written.
  8. Settle. Two of the three retrieved sources were actually cited, so only those two appear in the closing reference list — the third is dropped rather than listed as though it had contributed. The completed answer is written to both the transcript and the cache the next turn will read from. Stage timings and token counts are published.

End to end: about 2.6 seconds, of which roughly 63% elapsed before the first word existed. The clinician experienced a wait of about 1.9 seconds, because the remaining generation time disappeared into their reading time. That difference — 2.6 seconds of work felt as 1.9 seconds of waiting — is the entire argument for streaming.

What the same question costs on the tool branch

Had the clinician instead asked “which statins are covered on her plan?”, the first chunk would have carried a tool call rather than content. The visible stream is interrupted before anything reaches the screen, the streamed argument fragments are reassembled, the patient's plan is resolved, the covered-drug list is searched once per drug class in parallel, and the result is rendered as a table. That table is appended to the same prompt and sent to a second model call — one issued against a client with no tools bound to it, which is what makes a third hop structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged.

This branch is behind a feature flag and appears in none of the recorded traces, so the honest statement about its latency is that it is unmeasured. It pays for a second full model call plus the coverage lookup in between, and nobody has yet measured what that does to a clinician's wait.

Lanes A and B run concurrently, so they contribute the larger of the two rather than their sum. That single decision removes roughly 45% of the pre-model wait.

What this example does not show

Whether the answer was any good. Every number above is a latency measurement, and the system has no correctness signal at all — no grounding check, no retrieval recall measurement, no frozen regression set. It can tell you precisely how long a clinically wrong answer took to produce. That gap is named directly under the Settle stage rather than left for an interviewer to find.

The patient, the lab values and the conversation in this example are synthetic. Latency figures are medians across five recorded execution-timeline traces of one deployment and describe that deployment only. Nothing here is clinical guidance.
